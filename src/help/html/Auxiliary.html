Auxiliary

Contents

The Auxiliary component builds a shape that describes another component rather than part of the aircraft: the region a rotor's tips sweep, the zone a burst rotor's fragments fly through, the ground plane under a set of landing gear, a tire's spray, a pilot's field of view. Such shapes are implied by what the model already holds, and drawing them by hand would be tedious and would not follow the design as it changes. Each Mode computes its shape from the Auxiliary component's parent, so an Auxiliary component is always added as the child of the component it describes, and it follows that component: change the propeller or the gear, and the shape changes with it.

The shapes are used three ways: to inspect by eye, as a limit to move a part against with Snap To (in the Model menu), and as the geometry of the Geometry Analysis manager's checks.

Quick Start

  1. Select the component to be described -- a Propeller, a Gear, a pilot -- and add an Auxiliary component, so that it is that component's child.
  2. Choose its Mode on the Design tab. The box under Mode says what kind of parent the mode needs.
  3. Set the mode's parameters. For the ground modes, choose the bogies of the parent Gear that touch the ground.
  4. Use it as the geometry of an analysis in the Geometry Analysis manager.

A Clone of a Propeller or of a Gear serves as such a parent too.

An Auxiliary component is always attached to its parent's component axes, and it takes its parent's symmetry and flip, so it is mirrored wherever its parent is; these controls are greyed. Its own location and rotation on the XForm tab move it from the parent's origin and axes; leave them at zero unless the zone should sit elsewhere, such as at a rotor inside a nacelle. A rotor zone is built about its parent's XX axis, so for a parent whose axes do not lie along the rotor's shaft, hang it from a Blank placed at the rotor's center instead.

Zones that reach far away -- the rotor fragment and thrown blade paths, the tire spray, the debris zones, the ground planes -- are drawn out to 1.51.5 times the length of the diagonal of the model's bounding box, so they reach past the whole aircraft.

Rotor Tip Path

A keep-out zone: the region the blade tips of a flapping rotor can sweep, which no part of the aircraft may enter. It is the parent propeller's tips flapping about a hinge at r_flap/R from the shaft, by up to Theta Thrust toward the thrust and Theta Anti Thrust away from it, all the way around (Figure 1). With Automatic Diameter, the default, the diameter is the propeller's. With Automatic r_flap/R, also the default, r_flap/R is the radial place of the propeller's flap hinge (see Hinges); it is on in models saved by earlier versions too, so their tip paths now take the propeller's flap hinge. Turn it off and set r_flap/R again to keep an earlier tip path.

Figure 1. The tip path of the default propeller, cut by the plane through its shaft and its first blade, with its flap hinge moved to r/R 0.1 and both angles at their default, 15 degrees.

Rotor Burst

A keep-out zone for systems that a burst rotor must not disable: the zone its fragments are taken to pass through, a ring about the parent's XX axis that is Root Length wide at the shaft and widens outward by Theta Thrust toward −X-X and Theta Anti Thrust toward +X+X (Figure 2). Root Offset is the fraction of Root Length ahead of the parent's origin, 0.5 to center it. With Automatic Diameter and a propeller parent, the diameter is 1.51.5 times the length of the diagonal of the model's bounding box.

Figure 2. A rotor burst zone about the default propeller, cut by the plane through its shaft and its first blade, with Root Length 3 and a diameter of 45.

AC 20-128A Rotor Fragment

The path of a fragment thrown from a turbine rotor, as the FAA's AC 20-128A defines it [1], for the Risk Angle analysis. The fragment, Fragment Length long, leaves from its center of gravity at CG Radius from the parent's XX axis, tangent to its circle, at Release Angle measured from +Z+Z in the direction of rotation. It spreads by Forward toward −X-X and Aft toward +X+X as it goes (Figure 3). Rotate Direction checked is rotation about −X-X, as a propeller turns normally; unchecked, about +X+X.

Figure 3. A Generic Fragment from a rotor set back 2 from the nose of its pod, Release Angle 30, Forward 10 and Aft 5: from upstream (left), and from above (right). The dashed circle is the path of the fragment's center of gravity.

AC 25.905-1 Thrown Blade

The path of a propeller blade thrown from its hub, as the FAA's AC 25.905-1 defines it [2], for the Risk Angle analysis. It is the same shape as a rotor fragment, built from the parent propeller: the fragment is centered on the blade's center of gravity, at CG Fraction of the blade's length from its root, and is long enough to reach both the blade's root and its tip; the direction of rotation is the propeller's. Basic Blade uses a CG Fraction of 0.34 and a spread of 5∘5^\circ fore and aft; Unconventional Blade takes them as given.

Ground Planes

A Gear is its nominal ground plane: the ground with every bogie in its nominal state. The ground plane modes build off-nominal ground planes from it: the ground under chosen bogies of the parent Gear, each in a chosen state, and turned about their contact points or axles as an analysis requires (Figure 5). They draw the plane and those bogies' tires with it. Make one Auxiliary component for each condition to be checked.

For each bogie, I Symm chooses the bogie or its mirror image, for a Symmetrical bogie (Figure 4). Suspension Mode chooses the strut Nominal, Compressed or Extended: compressed moves the bogie's pivot by the Gear's Compression Travel along the suspension direction, toward the aircraft, and extended moves it the other way by the Extension Travel. Tire Mode chooses the tire's radius below the pivot: Static Load (the static loaded radius, the tire under the aircraft's weight), Unloaded (the unloaded tire's radius, as at first contact), Grown (the tire spinning at speed), or Flat (the radius of the wheel's flanges, which a flat tire rolls on). The bogie's contact point is its pivot, so moved, less that radius. The plane is the ground for that state of the gear, and each analysis reads the contact points and the Gear's center of gravity range through it.

Figure 4. The Design tab of the 3pt Ground Plane of Figure 5: the nose bogie with its strut Compressed and its tire Flat, and the symmetrical main bogie twice, I Symm 0 for one side and 1 for its mirror image.

Mode The plane Analyses
3pt Ground Plane Through the contact points of three bogies. Plane Min/Max Distance, Weight Distribution, Ground Maneuverability
2pt Ground Plane Through the line joining the contact points of two bogies, as level as that line allows. Plane 2pt Angle Contact, Tipback Angle, Tipover Angle
1pt Ground Plane Through the contact point of one bogie's outermost tire, level. Plane 1pt Angle Contact

The order in which the bogies are chosen does not matter; the plane's normal is always taken pointing up.

The angles of the 2pt and 1pt modes pose the plane drawn, so that a contact angle an analysis finds can be seen. Bogie Theta pitches bogies that have wheels in tandem about their pivots. Wheel Theta turns the 2pt plane about the line through the rear axles, for a positive angle, or the front axles, for a negative one, as the aircraft rotates onto its tail or its nose; on the 1pt plane it pitches the plane. Roll Theta turns the 1pt plane about the line through the contact point along XX, as the aircraft rolls onto a wing tip. A positive Roll Theta lowers the plane toward +Y+Y, as the aircraft rolls onto its left wing tip about a left bogie (Figure 6); about a right bogie, roll onto the right wing tip with a negative Roll Theta.

Figure 5. Two off-nominal ground planes of a tricycle gear, from the side, with the Gear's tires drawn in their nominal state. The 2pt Ground Plane rests on the main gear and is turned by Wheel Theta 12 about the rear axles, as for a tail strike; the 3pt Ground Plane has the nose tire Flat and the nose strut Compressed by 0.4, and so rises toward the nose.

Figure 6. A 1pt Ground Plane under the outer tire of the left main bogie, Roll Theta 8, from ahead. The plane turns about the line along XX through that tire's contact point and falls toward +Y+Y.

Single Gear

One bogie of the parent Gear, alone, in a chosen state, to check the room the gear needs: that the retracted gear fits its wheel well, with the Packaging Interference analysis, or that the extended gear clears the airframe, with External Interference. Gear Mode chooses it Down, Up, or Intermediate at Retract; I Symm and Suspension Mode are as above. Clearance Mode chooses the tire: Nominal, Grown (the tire spinning at speed, grown by the Tire and Rim Association's allowances [6]), or Clearance (the TRA's clearance envelope around the grown tire [6, 7]), which a wheel well must leave room for. The Gear page draws all three.

ESDU 83042a Tire Spray Envelope

The spray thrown up by a bogie's front tires running through standing water, after ESDU 83042a [3], so that engine intakes and other parts can be kept out of it. It is almost always attached to the nose gear. A side plume leaves from beside the footprint of each outermost front tire and runs aft, rising at the side elevation angle γs\gamma_s and spreading outward at the plan angle θs\theta_s (Figure 7). The incremental angle λs\lambda_s sets its width, which grows by tan⁡λs\tan\lambda_s for each unit the plume runs aft, and the inclination angle ϕs\phi_s leans its sides outward (Figure 8). A bogie with two or more wheels across and one in tandem also throws a center plume between each pair of neighboring wheels, rising at the center elevation angle γc\gamma_c, Δyc\Delta y_c wide.

The Tire Contact width bgb_g and half length hh, and Δyc\Delta y_c, are given as fractions of the tire diameter DD: Width (b_g/D), Half Length (h/D) and Δyc/D\Delta y_c/D. Each tire's footprint is bgb_g wide and 2h2h long, centered on its contact point. The plumes leave the ground hh ahead of the footprints, the side plumes bg/4b_g/4 outboard of them (Figure 9).

Figure 7. The side plumes and center plume of a nose bogie, two across and one in tandem, at the default angles, from the side (top) and from above (bottom). From above, the upper edges of the side plume on the +Y+Y side are solid and its edges on the ground dashed.

Figure 8. The plumes of Figure 7 from behind, cut square to XX far aft of the bogie.

Figure 9. Where the plumes of Figure 7 start, under the nose tires, from above.

The mode draws the envelope; it does not find it. ESDU 83042a gives the angles and widths from the aircraft's tire and operating conditions, such as tire pressure and ground speed; work them out with the ESDU method and enter them here. Then check the envelope with External Interference: that engine intakes are clear of the spray, and that it does not strike structure or systems it could damage.

AMC 25.734 Wheel and Tire Failure

The zones that debris from a failed wheel or tire can reach, after EASA AMC 25.734 and the wheel and tire failure model it adopts [4, 8], for the bogie chosen. They are keep-out zones for the systems a failure must not disable, such as hydraulic and fuel lines, checked with External Interference:

Model Zone
Model 1 Large Large tire debris: a fan above the axle in the plane of the wheels, spreading 15∘15^\circ to each side; a strip along the ground ahead of the tires and a wedge below 45∘45^\circ behind them are cut from it as negative volumes.
Model 1 Small Small tire debris: as Model 1 Large, spreading 30∘30^\circ to each side.
Model 2 Wheel flange fragments: from the outer side of each outermost wheel, a cone about the axle starting at the flange diameter and opening at 20∘20^\circ from the axle.
Model 3E A flailing tire strip, with the gear extended: a ring about each tire, as far out as a strip 2.52.5 shoulder widths long can reach.
Model 3R As Model 3E, with the gear retracted.

Model 1's zone is the fan less its two negative volumes (Figure 10); the interference analysis, like CompGeom, removes them.

Figure 10. Model 1 Large on a main bogie with two axles, from the side: a fan above each axle (shaded), and the strip along the ground ahead of the tires and the wedge below 45 degrees behind them (dashed), which are cut from it.

3pt Composite Clearance Envelope

A clearance envelope, read from a file, swept along the ground under the aircraft, for the Composite Clearance Envelope analysis. The envelope is a profile across the aircraft's track, such as the obstacles a taxiing aircraft must clear. It is swept along the line from the contact point of the Nose Gear Bogie toward the midpoint of Main Bogie 1 and Main Bogie 2, in the plane of the three contact points, with its origin at the nose gear's contact point.

Read File reads the profile: a text file of two numbers on each line, the lateral position and the height of each point of the profile, in order. A positive lateral position is toward the Gear's +Y+Y, the aircraft's right as seen from behind, looking forward (Figure 11); a profile taken from a drawing seen from ahead has the signs of its lateral positions reversed. Envelope Length Units is the unit of the file, or Consistent for the model's own units; the parent Gear's Model Units convert it.

Such envelopes come from the equipment an aircraft must taxi over or be launched from. The composite clearance envelope of a carrier's nose gear catapult launching system [9], for example, is a profile across the catapult track, in inches, of the deck fittings to either side that the airframe must clear. NASA CR-175104 [10] shows such an envelope applied to an aircraft design.

Figure 11. An example envelope under a tricycle gear, seen from behind, looking forward: deck fittings at three heights to the right of the track and one to the left, which the airframe must clear. The file's lateral positions and heights are in inches; the Gear's Model Units are feet.

The aircraft need not sit square on its track. With the nose gear on the track, the main gear may be off its center: a catapult launching system allows the main gear's center up to 24 in off the track in the hookup position [9, 10]. Main Gear Offset represents this. It turns the track about the nose gear's contact point, toward Main Bogie 2 for a positive offset, so that the main gear's center lies the offset to the other side of the track, measured square to the track (Figure 12). It is in the Gear's Model Units, not the file's.

Figure 12. Main Gear Offset 2 on the tricycle gear, from above, as the aircraft sits on the track: the track straight, the aircraft turned about its nose gear's contact point, and the main gear's center 2 off the track, measured square to it.

Super Cone

A cone from a point, whose cross section is any cross-section curve: for example a pilot's field of view, for the From Point Visibility analysis, which uses the cone's apex as its viewpoint and the cone as its field of view. The Profile is drawn in degrees: its width is azimuth and its height elevation, seen from the apex looking forward along −X-X, positive azimuth toward +Y+Y and positive elevation up. The default profile is the AC 25.773 pilot vision envelope [5] (Figure 13); the other curves are described in XSecCurves.

Figure 13. The default Super Cone profile, the AC 25.773 pilot vision envelope for the left seat, in azimuth and elevation.

The profile is read as azimuth and elevation and projected onto a sphere about the apex, so its outline is a set of directions (Figure 14), and the cone reaches out to the distance the other zones are drawn to. The apex is the parent's origin, or the eye point of a Human parent. World aligns the cone with the model's axes, whatever the parent's rotation; Object aligns it with the parent's axes, or the Human's head. For any other point or direction, hang the Super Cone from a Blank.

Figure 14. The default Super Cone on a seated Human: every direction from the design eye inside the profile of Figure 13, out to the cone's far edge. The straight lines run from the eye to the profile's corners.

The cone also marks surfaces. An Intersect sub-surface on the fuselage (see Sub-Surface Types) whose Intersect Geom is a pilot's Super Cone outlines the part of the fuselage the required view passes through: the region a windshield must cover.

Shared Tabs

The Gen, XForm, Mass and Sub tabs are common to every component; see Gen, XForm, Mass and Sub-Surfaces.

Scripting

A script adds an Auxiliary component with AddGeom( "AUXILIARY", parent_id ) and chooses its mode with the parm AuxiliaryGeomType (AUX_GEOM_MODE) in the group Design, where its other parms are too. The bogies of the ground, gear and spray modes are set with SetAuxiliaryGeomContactPtID( geom_id, index, bogie_id ) and read with GetAuxiliaryGeomContactPtID, index 0, 1 or 2; ReadAuxiliaryGeomCCEFile reads a clearance envelope.
