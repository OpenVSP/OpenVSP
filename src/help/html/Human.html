Human

Contents

The Human component models a person: a pilot, a passenger or a ground crew member, sized from population statistics and posed joint by joint. Its shape comes from a statistical model of adult body scans, so a few numbers -- sex, stature, body mass index, age and sitting height -- give a complete, realistic body. A skeleton inside the body then bends it into any pose from standing to seated, and seventeen preset postures, most of them from published measurements, give a start. The result is a closed triangle mesh, or a set of closed surfaces, with a mass, a volume and a design eye point, for checking fit, reach, visibility and mass properties.

Quick Start

A standing person

  1. On the Anthropometric tab choose Gender, then set Length Unit and Mass Unit to the units of the model.
  2. Set % Stature and % BMI, or type the Stature and Mass directly.
  3. Leave the pose as it is: a new human stands.
  4. Place it with the XForm tab. The origin is the waist joint, so set the location of the waist, not the feet.

A seated occupant

  1. Choose Gender, then set Length Unit and Mass Unit to the units of the model.
  2. Set the size, as for a standing person.
  3. On the Pose tab choose a seated Preset (SEATED ERECT, PASSENGER, DRIVER, PILOT, ...) and click Set, then adjust the joints to fit the seat.
  4. Place it with the XForm tab, by the waist joint (Figure 3), and pitch it with YRot for a reclined seat. Attach a Super Cone Auxiliary to it to show the field of view (see Design Eye).

Representation

Shape chooses what the human is made of.

  • MESH, the default, is the triangle mesh of the body model itself.
  • SURFACE is twenty overlapping closed surfaces: trunk, head and neck, and on each side an arm, a palm, a thumb, four fingers, a leg and a foot (Figure 1). Each part runs on inside its neighbour, so their union is the closed body; CompGeom and CFD Mesh find where they cross, as they do for a wing on a fuselage. The surfaces are smooth everywhere but at the closed ends of each part. They follow the mesh to within about half a millimeter typically and a few millimeters nearly everywhere; the largest differences are where the posed body presses into itself, as at the groin when seated. The toes are joined, so the foot's surface bridges the gaps between them. The eyes are fixed points of the head's surface.

Figure 1. A human as SURFACE, each part in its own color: the trunk, the head and neck, and on each side an arm, a palm, a thumb, four fingers, a leg and a foot.

Both are built from the same body size and the same skeleton, and pose together, through the same tetrahedral mesh (see How the Body Follows). The Volume and the density from Auto Density are the mesh's in either case; the union of the surfaces holds the same volume to within a fraction of a percent (0.2% for the default human). Use SURFACE where a surface is needed (see The Human in Analyses) or for smooth exports such as STEP and IGES; use MESH otherwise.

In SURFACE, Num_U and Num_W on the Gen tab are the number of points along and around each patch of the surfaces, 3 by default.

Units

Length Unit and Mass Unit say what units the human is built in. They are not display units: the body is built at its stature in the length unit, so a 1.756 m man is 1.756 model units tall with Length Unit M and 5.76 with Length Unit FT. Set them to the units the rest of the model uses. The default is FT and LBM.

Changing the length unit keeps the person the same size in the real world: the stature percentile stays, and the stature is restated in the new unit. Changing the mass unit restates the mass.

The XForm tab's Scale has no effect on a human; its size is set by the stature and the length unit alone.

Body Size

The body shape is the statistical body shape model of Human Shapes (humanshape.org), by Matthew P. Reed of the University of Michigan Transportation Research Institute (UMTRI), used with permission [1, 2]. The model was fitted to laser scans of several hundred adults by principal component analysis, as UMTRI's body shape models are [3], and predicts the whole body, skin and joint centers, from a few anthropometric inputs: stature, body mass index, the ratio of sitting height to stature, and age. Every point of the body is a linear function of those inputs and of the product of BMI and age, so a change in any of them moves the body smoothly. Between them, the controls on the Anthropometric tab describe most of the variation in body shape among adults.

Stature and BMI

Stature and BMI each have a value and a percentile; set either and the other follows. The percentiles come from the United States National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) of adults aged 20 and over, 2011-2014, separately for men and women [4]:

Percentile Male stature, mm Female stature, mm Male BMI Female BMI
5th 1634 1498 20.7 19.6
50th 1756 1619 27.7 27.7
95th 1881 1735 39.8 43.3

Between the tabulated percentiles (5, 10, 15, 25, 50, 75, 85, 90 and 95) the value is interpolated smoothly. Switching Gender keeps the percentiles, so a 95th percentile man becomes a 95th percentile woman, and shorter. The limits on Stature are the 5th and 95th percentile of the chosen sex.

These are percentiles of the general adult population. A population chosen for an occupation differs: military aircrew, for example, are selected for size and are leaner. For such a population set Stature and Mass (or BMI) from its own survey, such as ANSUR II for the U.S. Army [5]; the percentile controls then report where that person falls among the general population.

Mass, BMI and stature are tied together by

BMI=mh2 \mathrm{BMI} = \frac{m}{h^2}

with the mass mm in kilograms and the stature hh in meters. Setting Mass sets BMI; setting BMI or % BMI sets the mass. Changing the stature keeps the BMI and changes the mass, so a taller person of the same build is heavier.

Age and sitting height

Age and Sitting H / H change the shape without changing the stature. A larger sitting height ratio gives a longer trunk and shorter legs. Age changes the build, by an amount that depends on the BMI. Leave both at their defaults unless the person in question is known to differ.

Volume and density

Volume is the volume of the closed body as posed, in the length unit cubed. With Auto Density on, the human's density on the Mass tab is set to Mass / Volume, so the mass properties of the model carry the person's mass at the right place, whatever the pose. Turn it off to set a density by hand.

The volume changes with the pose, because the flesh at a deeply bent joint is compressed: seated, the default human's volume is 6-7% less than standing, and squatting up to 11% less (see How the Body Follows). With Auto Density on the mass stays the person's and the density rises to match.

Origin and Axes

The human faces −X-X, with ZZ up and the person's right side toward +Y+Y, the way a pilot sits in an aircraft whose nose points along −X-X (Figure 2). The origin is the waist joint, and it stays there whatever the pose: the legs hang from it standing and fold in front of it seated (Figure 3). Place a seated occupant by the waist joint, not by the seat or the feet.

Figure 2. A new human, its origin at the waist joint, and its axes. The person faces −X-X; the right hand is toward +Y+Y.

Figure 3. The default human standing, as it is made, and in the Sitting preset. The origin is the same point of the body in both.

Presets

The pose bends the body at the joints of its skeleton. Preset and Set at the top of the Pose tab put the human into a standard posture (Figures 4-5): Set replaces every joint angle and turns R/L Sym on or off as the posture needs. Choose the preset first and then adjust. In a script, SetHumanPresetPose does the same, with the enum in the table below (see Scripting). A new human stands, in the Standing preset.

The postures are taken from published measurements where there are any; the others are drawn to match a named posture and are marked illustrative. A preset sets the joints only. Lying, crawling or reclined postures are made by pitching the whole body on the XForm tab: YRot -90 pitches the body face down, as for crawling, and a positive YRot reclines it.

The two neutral body postures take the published joint angles, but for the rotation of the upper arms (Shoulder IE 30), which is set to match the published figure of the posture. A neutral body posture is the posture the body relaxes into in weightlessness; it is the reference for workstation heights and reach on a spacecraft.

Joint Angles

Every angle is in degrees. The names are the anatomical ones: AB/AD is abduction (away from the body's centerline) and adduction, FE is flexion and extension, IE is internal and external rotation. The Pose tab holds them in groups: Torso and Head, then Limbs, where each arm and leg joint is there twice, in a Right Arm and Left Arm column and a Right Leg and Left Leg column, Parms ending in Rt and Lt; the labels of the right column name each row for both (Figure 6).

Figure 6. The Pose tab of a new human: the preset, the method, the torso and head, and the limbs. ARAP's settings are greyed while the method is FAST, and the left side while R/L Sym is on.

The left angles mean the same motions as the right: a positive Shoulder AB/AD raises either arm out to its own side, and the left side posed as the right is the mirror image of it. With R/L Sym on, the toggle on the Limbs divider and the default, the left side copies the right and its controls are greyed; turn it off to pose the sides apart.

The ranges broadly follow the normal ranges of motion of adults measured clinically [14], widened where a preset posture needs it. A pose inside the ranges can still put one part of the body through another (see Collisions).

The head turns at the base of the neck, and the neck bends smoothly with it.

The shoulder angles are measured from the arm hanging straight down, so with every other angle zero, Shoulder AB/AD 90 holds the arm straight out to the side and Shoulder FE 90 straight forward, whatever the body's size (Figure 9). The default arm hangs 20 degrees out from the body, as the body model's arms do. Shoulder AB/AD and FE together swing the arm by one rotation, along the shortest arc from hanging, toward a direction between the side and the front: AB/AD 45 with FE 45 raises the arm about 64 degrees, midway between the two planes. So neither angle loses its meaning anywhere in its range, including with the arm straight forward or straight up. Shoulder IE then turns the upper arm about its own length, and the forearm and hand with it. It is easiest to see with the elbow bent: internal rotation swings the forearm in across the body, as in Figure 9.

Raising an arm also moves the shoulder, as a real shoulder girdle does. The clavicle and shoulder blade turn in proportion to the arm's elevation from hanging, following the shoulder rhythm of the upper extremity model of Holzbaur, Murray and Delp [15], which they took from the measurements of de Groot and Brand [16]. The shoulder joint rises and moves back and in toward the neck as the arm goes up, and the top of the shoulder rises with it. The rhythm follows the arm's elevation only, so lowering the arm returns the shoulder to its place.

Elbow is the angle between the upper arm and the forearm, zero for a straight arm. The elbow bends the forearm forward in the plane of the upper arm, keeping the slight outward angle of the rest forearm (the carrying angle). Forearm turns the forearm and hand about the forearm's length; at zero the palm faces the thigh. It turns the palm down when the elbow is bent 90 degrees, and toward the back when the arm hangs. Wrist FE bends the hand at the wrist toward the palm (flexion) or toward its back (extension). Wrist Deviation then swings it sideways in the plane of the palm, toward the little finger (ulnar deviation) or the thumb (radial deviation): with the palm against the thigh, a positive angle swings the fingertips back. The signs follow the joint coordinate system recommended by the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) [17]: flexion, ulnar deviation and pronation are positive.

Figure 9. Positive shoulder, elbow, forearm and wrist angles. Shoulder AB/AD, Forearm and Wrist FE are seen from in front of the person and move the right arm; Shoulder FE, Elbow and Wrist Deviation are seen from the person's left and move the left arm; Shoulder IE is seen from above and moves the right arm, its elbow at 90. The tint shows the arm as it was.

The hip follows the order of the ISB joint coordinate system for the hip [18]: the leg first swings forward by Hip FE, then out by Hip AB/AD from wherever Hip FE left it, and then turns about its length by Hip IE. So in a seated pose Hip AB/AD spreads the knees by its own angle, and Hip IE swings the lower legs: internal rotation of the thigh swings the foot outward (Figure 10).

Figure 10. Positive hip, knee and ankle angles. Hip AB/AD and Hip IE are seen from in front of the person and move the right leg; Hip FE, Knee and Ankle are seen from the person's left and move the left leg.

Collisions

The skeleton does not check for collisions, so a pose can put an arm through the body or a leg through the other; check a demanding pose by eye. Where body parts press together, as the belly on the thighs when seated or the calf on the thigh when squatting, the skin of the two overlaps rather than flattening against each other.

Files saved by earlier versions of OpenVSP keep their joint angles, which now mean the motions described here: the shoulder and hip angles are measured from the vertical and combined as above, and wrist flexion is positive. A pose saved by an earlier version may need adjusting.

How the Body Follows

The skin follows the bones smoothly, so a bent elbow or hip deforms the flesh around it rather than hinging a rigid part. To do so the body is filled with a tetrahedral mesh: about 37,000 tetrahedra on a 40 mm lattice inside the skin, with the skin's vertices and points along each bone among their corners. The mesh is built once, for every body of both sexes, and is mirror symmetric, so a symmetric pose gives a symmetric body. Its corners move with the body size, as the skin does.

To pose the body the points along each bone are moved with their bone, and the rest of the tetrahedral mesh is deformed to follow them. A bone that twists, such as the upper arm, the forearm, the thigh, the spine and the neck, spreads its twist along its length. The skin and, in SURFACE, the surfaces' control points ride in the tetrahedra that hold them, so the mesh and the surfaces are posed alike.

Method chooses how the tetrahedral mesh is deformed; the settings of the other method are greyed.

  • FAST, the default, is Fast Automatic Skinning Transformations [19]. Each point of the mesh blends the movements of the bones with weights that fall off smoothly through the body, bounded biharmonic weights [20], and with extra weights whose movements FAST solves for so the deformation stays as close to rigid as it can. FAST It. is the number of iterations, 30 by default. It is fast enough to pose while dragging a slider: a few milliseconds per pose for the default human.
  • ARAP is volumetric as-rigid-as-possible deformation [21, 22]. The points on the bones are held where their bones put them, and every other point of the mesh moves freely, so that each tetrahedron turns as nearly rigidly as it can. It iterates until no point of the skin moves more than ARAP Tol (in millimeters, 0.2 by default) over ten iterations, or until ARAP It. iterations (100 by default). It takes about a tenth of a second per pose for the default human, some thirty times FAST's time.

The two agree in moderate poses. ARAP keeps the body's volume better in deep flexion: squatting, the default human loses about 4% of its volume with ARAP and 11% with FAST; reaching overhead, under 1% with ARAP and nearly 4% with FAST. Use FAST while posing, and ARAP for a final pose that is deeply bent, or where the volume and mass distribution matter.

Both methods are iterative, and their result is an approximation that depends on how many iterations they run. ARAP's tolerance measures how far the skin moves over ten iterations, not how far it is from the shape ARAP would settle on, so in an extreme pose ARAP can stop while still changing slowly. At the default settings the seated human stops within about 5 mm of the shape ARAP settles on, but the crawling one stops more than 150 mm from it across the belly, and one with both arms overhead about 90 mm from it at the upper back. Raising ARAP It. or lowering ARAP Tol brings ARAP closer to its settled shape, at a cost in time, and in some poses it does not settle at all. Neither method keeps the skin from passing through itself where the body folds deeply (the groin when seated, the armpit with the arms raised), and with both arms overhead the base of the neck narrows somewhat. The results are repeatable: the same body, pose and settings give the same shape, whatever pose came before.

Both are computed with libigl [23].

Design Eye

The human carries a design eye point between the eyes (Figure 2), which moves and turns with the head. Two things use it:

  • A Super Cone Auxiliary component whose parent is a human is placed at the design eye and turned with the head, so it shows the field of view from the person's eyes.
  • From Point Visibility in Geometry Analysis can take a human as its viewpoint, and looks from the design eye.

With symmetry, each copy of the human has its own design eye. An attached Super Cone follows the eye as the human is posed or resized.

The Human in Analyses

As a MESH, a human is a closed triangle mesh, the same in every analysis. CompGeom, Mass Properties, the slicing analyses, interference checks and the mesh exports all use it, and VSPAERO takes it as one component. Its mesh is fixed by the model: the Gen tab's tessellation controls do not apply. It is not a surface, so it has no sub-surfaces, its degenerate geometry is empty, and it takes no part where a surface is needed: CFD Mesh, FEA Mesh, Fit Model, or as the boundary or direction of a projection.

As a SURFACE, a human is like any component made of surfaces: CompGeom, Mass Properties and CFD Mesh work on the union of its parts, the exports include STEP and IGES, its degenerate geometry has one entry per part, and VSPAERO takes each part as a component. The screens that choose a component for a projection, Fit Model or an FEA fixed point still leave humans out.

Symmetry and Flip on the XForm tab work as for any component; see Symmetry and Flip.

Scripting

A human is built and posed through its Parms, in the groups Anthropometric and Pose, with the names in the tables above; one function, SetHumanPresetPose, puts it in a preset. Shape is Representation in the Design group, HUMAN_REP_MESH or HUMAN_REP_SURF; the surfaces are in the order torso, head, then each right part followed by its left one (arm, palm, thumb, the fingers from the index, leg, foot). Method is PoseMethod in the Pose group, HUMAN_POSE_ARAP or HUMAN_POSE_FAST, with FASTIter, ARAPMaxIter and ARAPTol. R/L Sym is RLSym.

SetHumanPresetPose poses a human in a preset, as Set does: it sets RLSym and every pose angle, and keeps the method, its settings and the body's size. The preset is one of the enums in the preset table above (HUMAN_PRESET_POSE). Call Update afterward. Each angle can then be adjusted with SetParmVal, as on the Pose tab. ShowSkelFlag in the Anthropometric group draws the skeleton with the feature lines, unposed in black and posed in blue; it has no control on the screen.

import openvsp as vsp

h = vsp.AddGeom( "HUMAN" )
vsp.SetParmVal( h, "LenUnit", "Anthropometric", vsp.LEN_M )
vsp.SetParmVal( h, "Stature_pct", "Anthropometric", 0.95 )

# Seated as a driver, the head turned 20 degrees to the left
vsp.SetHumanPresetPose( h, vsp.HUMAN_PRESET_DRIVER )
vsp.SetParmVal( h, "RotateHead", "Pose", -20.0 )

vsp.Update()

The same in AngelScript:

string h = AddGeom( "HUMAN" );
SetParmVal( h, "LenUnit", "Anthropometric", LEN_M );
SetParmVal( h, "Stature_pct", "Anthropometric", 0.95 );

// Seated as a driver, the head turned 20 degrees to the left
SetHumanPresetPose( h, HUMAN_PRESET_DRIVER );
SetParmVal( h, "RotateHead", "Pose", -20.0 );

Update();
